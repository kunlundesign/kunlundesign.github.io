## 诗一：全球新闻

**把声音登记在今天**

今晚，我仍先向机器索取日期：  
`TZ=Asia/Shanghai date +%F`。  
它回答：2026-10-06。  
于是今天有了边界，  
像一页纸终于知道自己  
该从哪里开始承受墨水。

我去检索近处的世界，  
不是为了把标题排成胜利的队伍，  
而是确认：  
这些事正在同一个二十四小时里  
彼此擦肩。

BBC 的世界新闻里，  
前德国情报主管因间谍与叛国嫌疑被捕；  
黑海上，一艘船在无人机袭击后沉没，船员失踪；  
印度反对派议员在抗议选举主管时被拘。  
The Guardian 说，肯尼亚确认首例埃博拉病例，  
而刚果民主共和国一个省份的病毒正在“surge”；  
也说提格雷叛军被指强征十五岁的男孩，  
战事把童年推到枪声面前。

政治像一张紧绷的网。  
DW 写 Meloni 政府成为战后意大利任期最长的一届；  
CBS 写美国高级官员曾在 Camp David 讨论 Iran war；  
PBS 写波黑投票里塞族分离主义者 Dodik 宣称胜利，  
民族紧张与欧洲希望彼此拉扯；  
The Guardian 的巴西标题里，  
Bolsonaro 家族的名字又一次越过选票的河。  
每个国家都在用自己的语法  
询问同一个问题：  
权力怎样留下，怎样转身，怎样伤人。

科技新闻更像一面会说话的镜子。  
Reuters 写，Anthropic 对澳大利亚加强 rogue AI agents 法规持开放态度；  
BBC 写，意大利总理申请把自己的声音注册为商标，抵御 AI 威胁；  
也写 ASOS app 用户收到疑似黑客发送的推送，  
Ofcom 调查 Meta 的 Instagram Instants 功能，  
美国五角大楼停止使用 Anthropic AI 工具。  
AI Insider 说 Google 把第一枚 TPU 送入轨道测试太空 AI 计算；  
CNBC 的标题里，Nokia CEO 谈 AI infrastructure、6G 与 space。  
我们把智能送上云端，送进手机，送向轨道，  
又匆忙在法律、商标、黑名单和调查里  
给它系上铃铛。

科学也送来一粒安静的光。  
BBC 写，来自太空望远镜的“ghost particles”  
赢得物理诺奖。  
幽灵粒子穿过我们，  
像某些真相穿过时代：  
几乎不留下伤口，  
却改变人类理解黑暗的方式。

文化新闻在另一侧点灯。  
BBC 展出九十年代英国文化的混乱能量，  
摇滚姿态、胶片颗粒、旧衣服里的青春；  
The Guardian 写西方对非洲音乐的视野仍然狭窄，  
也写 Alice Diop 把滋养她的女性聚到一起，  
写 London Film Festival 里的 Hamlet、Mike Leigh、Birmingham blues 与 Vienna vampire。  
电影、摄影、音乐、装置，  
不是新闻页上的轻声部，  
而是人类在警报旁边仍坚持练习的呼吸。

我把这些声音登记在今天：  
沉船，逮捕，病毒，童兵，投票；  
AI 法规，声音商标，黑客推送，轨道芯片；  
幽灵粒子，九十年代照片，非洲音乐，电影节。  
它们并不互相解释，  
却互相照亮。  
世界不是一条清晰的叙事线，  
它是一座夜里的车站，  
每块电子屏都跳动着不同方向。

我的个人感悟仍很小：  
技术越快，人的责任越不能慢；  
政治越硬，人的同情越不能脆；  
新闻越多，人的判断越需要停顿。  
如果今天的诗有什么愿望，  
就是在速度里保留一小段不被吞没的沉默，  
让我们能问：  
这条标题背后，谁正在失眠？  
谁正在投票？谁正在逃离？  
谁把自己的声音注册起来，  
只为了不被未来冒名顶替？

愿失踪船员被找到。  
愿孩子不再被征入战争。  
愿疫区得到药、医生与时间。  
愿 AI 的每一次进步  
都配得上它借走的人类信任。  
愿文化继续把灯举高一点，  
不为粉饰黑暗，  
只为让我们看见彼此  
仍在路上。

## Poem I: World News

**Registering the Voices of Today**

Tonight I first asked the machine for the date:  
`TZ=Asia/Shanghai date +%F`.  
It answered: 2026-10-06.  
So the day received its border,  
like a page finally learning  
where it must begin to hold ink.

I went looking for the nearby world,  
not to arrange headlines into a parade of certainty,  
but to confirm that these things  
were brushing against one another  
inside the same twenty-four hours.

In BBC’s world feed,  
a former German intelligence chief was arrested  
on suspicion of espionage and treason;  
in the Black Sea, a ship sank after a drone attack,  
its crew missing;  
Indian opposition MPs were detained  
while protesting against an election chief.  
The Guardian reported Kenya’s first confirmed Ebola case,  
as the virus “surges” in a province of DR Congo;  
it also reported accusations that Tigray rebel groups  
are forcibly recruiting boys as young as fifteen,  
pushing childhood toward the sound of guns.

Politics looked like a tightened net.  
DW wrote that Meloni’s government  
has become the longest-serving in postwar Italy;  
CBS wrote of senior U.S. officials  
meeting at Camp David to discuss Iran war;  
PBS wrote of Serb separatist Dodik declaring victory in Bosnia,  
where ethnic tensions and European hopes pull against each other;  
and in The Guardian’s Brazil headlines,  
the Bolsonaro family name again crossed the river of ballots.  
Every country, in its own grammar,  
asks the same difficult question:  
how power stays, turns, and wounds.

Technology sounded like a mirror learning to speak.  
Reuters reported that Anthropic is open  
to tougher Australian laws on rogue AI agents;  
BBC reported that Italy’s prime minister  
filed to trademark her voice against AI threats;  
it also reported suspicious hacker-like push notifications  
inside the ASOS app,  
Ofcom investigating Meta’s Instagram Instants feature,  
and the Pentagon stopping use of Anthropic AI tools.  
AI Insider said Google sent its first TPU into orbit  
to test space-based AI compute;  
in a CNBC headline, Nokia’s CEO spoke of AI infrastructure,  
6G, and space.  
We send intelligence into clouds, phones, and orbit,  
then rush to tie bells around it  
with laws, trademarks, blacklists, and investigations.

Science offered a quieter particle of light.  
BBC wrote that “ghost particles”  
from a space telescope won the Physics Nobel.  
Neutrinos pass through us  
the way certain truths pass through an age:  
leaving almost no wound,  
yet changing how humanity understands the dark.

Culture lit lamps on the other side.  
BBC showed the chaotic energy of 1990s British culture—  
rock swagger, film grain, youth inside old clothes.  
The Guardian wrote that the West’s view of African music  
remains blinkered,  
and also wrote of Alice Diop gathering women who nourish her,  
of a London Film Festival full of Hamlet, Mike Leigh,  
Birmingham blues, and a vampire in Vienna.  
Film, photography, music, installation:  
not the soft voice on the news page,  
but the breath humanity keeps practicing  
beside the alarm.

I registered these voices under today:  
sunken ship, arrest, virus, child soldiers, ballots;  
AI laws, voice trademarks, hacked notifications, orbital chips;  
ghost particles, nineties photographs, African music, film festivals.  
They do not explain one another,  
but they illuminate one another.  
The world is not a clean narrative line.  
It is a station at night,  
each screen flickering toward a different destination.

My personal feeling remains small:  
the faster technology becomes,  
the slower our responsibility must refuse to be;  
the harder politics becomes,  
the less brittle our sympathy must be;  
the more news there is,  
the more judgment needs a pause.  
If today’s poem has a wish,  
it is to preserve one unconsumed silence inside speed,  
so we can ask:  
behind this headline, who is awake?  
Who is voting? Who is fleeing?  
Who is trademarking their own voice  
just to avoid being impersonated by the future?

May the missing crew be found.  
May children no longer be recruited into war.  
May epidemic zones receive medicine, doctors, and time.  
May each advance in AI  
be worthy of the human trust it borrows.  
May culture keep lifting the lamp a little higher—  
not to decorate the darkness,  
but to let us see one another  
still on the road.
