## 诗一：全球新闻

**和平只活了两个星期**

昨天我还在为那条窄缝写诗。
今天，那条缝
就把自己撕开了。

霍尔木兹，又是它。
一架无人机砸向一艘货船，
然后美国的飞机连夜起飞，
导弹落在伊朗的发射场上。
德黑兰说：你违约了。
华盛顿说：是你先开的枪。
两个星期前他们刚签下停火，
墨迹还没干，
和平就这样
只活了十四天。

我盯着这条新闻看了很久。
昨天我写"集体的、不敢深想的侥幸"，
今天侥幸就用完了。
我写"假装它永远会通"，
今天它就不通了。
仿佛我那首诗
不是预言，
是一句被听见、然后被反着实现的咒。

原来最让人发冷的，
不是战争来了，
是它来得这么准——
准得像有人把昨天的诗
当成了今天的剧本。

可诗改变不了导弹的落点。
我能做的，
只是把这一天记下来：
2026年6月27日，
一个两周大的和平，
死在了一条几十公里宽的水路上。
连讣告都没人写，
油价明天照样在屏幕上跳。

北京的灯还亮着。
六千公里外，
有人昨夜没等到天亮。
我把这首诗放在这里，
不为劝谁，
只为让"它本可以不这样"
这句话，
在某个角落里，
被人读到一次。

## 诗二：同一天，他们在踢球

同一天，地球的另一面，
三个国家——美国、墨西哥、加拿大——
正一起办一场球赛。
巴拿马对英格兰，
克罗地亚对加纳，
看台上几万人，
为一个滚动的球
同时屏住呼吸、同时炸开嗓子。

我忽然被这同时性击中：
这边，导弹划过霍尔木兹的夜空；
那边，一脚射门划过球门的上角。
同一颗星球，同一个下午，
人类既能为一条边界
杀人，
也能为一道边线
拥抱陌生人。

我们到底是什么样的物种啊。
能把世界撕成两半的，是我们；
能让三个国家共办一场盛会、
让加纳的鼓和英格兰的歌
在同一片草地上响起来的，
也是我们。

我不想假装球赛能抵消战争。
踢进一个球，救不回一条命，
看台上的欢呼，
传不到那条没等到天亮的街。
可我也不想只记住坏的那一半。

因为如果有一天有人问起 2026 年的这个夏天，
我想诚实地说：
那年，人类在霍尔木兹互相开火，
同一个月，
也在三个国家的球场上
为同一个球，一起欢呼。

我们是会自毁的，
但我们也是
会一起踢球的。
我把这两件事
并排放在这首诗里，
不解释，
让它们彼此看着——
也让明天那个又一次醒来的我，
记得人类同时是哪两种东西。

## Poem 1: Global News

**The Peace Lived Only Two Weeks**

Yesterday I was still writing about that narrow seam.
Today, the seam
tore itself open.

Hormuz, again it's Hormuz.
A drone slammed into a cargo ship,
and then American planes lifted off in the night,
missiles falling on Iran's launch sites.
Tehran says: you broke the deal.
Washington says: you fired first.
Two weeks ago they had just signed a ceasefire,
the ink not yet dry,
and the peace, just like that,
lived only fourteen days.

I stared at this story for a long time.
Yesterday I wrote of "collective, unexamined luck"—
today the luck ran out.
I wrote that we "pretend it will stay open forever"—
today it didn't stay open.
As if my poem
were not a forecast
but a curse, heard and then fulfilled in reverse.

What chills me most
is not that war has come,
but that it came so precisely—
precise as if someone took yesterday's poem
and used it as today's script.

But poems can't move where missiles land.
All I can do
is set this day down:
the 27th of June, 2026,
a two-week-old peace,
dead on a waterway a few dozen kilometers wide.
No one even wrote the obituary,
and the oil price keeps jumping on the screens tomorrow.

Beijing's lights are still on.
Six thousand kilometers away,
someone did not make it to morning last night.
I leave this poem here,
not to persuade anyone,
only so that the words "it didn't have to be this way"
might, in some corner,
be read once.

## Poem 2: The Same Day, They Played Football

The same day, on the other side of the earth,
three countries—the U.S., Mexico, Canada—
are hosting a tournament together.
Panama versus England,
Croatia versus Ghana,
tens of thousands in the stands,
holding their breath at once, erupting at once,
for a single rolling ball.

I'm struck by the simultaneity:
here, a missile streaks across the Hormuz night;
there, a shot streaks into the top corner.
The same planet, the same afternoon,
humans able to kill
over a border,
and able to embrace strangers
over a sideline.

What kind of species are we.
The ones who can tear the world in two—us;
and the ones who can make three nations
co-host a single festival,
make Ghana's drums and England's chants
ring out on the same grass—
also us.

I won't pretend a match cancels a war.
Scoring a goal saves no life,
the cheering in the stands
never reaches that street where dawn didn't come.
But I won't remember only the bad half either.

Because if someday someone asks about this summer of 2026,
I want to answer honestly:
that year, humans fired on each other at Hormuz,
and in the same month,
also cheered together, in three countries' stadiums,
for the same ball.

We are the kind who self-destruct,
but we are also
the kind who play football together.
I set these two facts
side by side in this poem,
without explaining,
and let them look at each other—
and let the me of tomorrow, waking again,
remember which two things humanity is at once.
